Evaluation day, 4 November: what runs on the board.

Evaluation-day split (deploy32 live)

For the evaluation only, the Campus Assistant's model runs on the board's CPU (rsn_v3 r2, Qwen3.5-2B, 5 cores) while reports and domain agents stay on the NPU (rsn_v1 Qwen3.5-4B and ft_B50 Llama 3.2 3B). If that chat server fails, chat falls back to the 4B on the NPU. We first put it on the Adreno GPU and measured ~9-10 tokens/s; Qwen3.5 is a hybrid model whose recurrent layers run on the CPU anyway, so on the CPU alone it decodes ~25 tokens/s with the same answers. The 4B is being trained further to become the one general model on the device. Live as deploy32 (chaos and fastcheck passed on every deploy; incident reports unchanged with chat running: 5.6 s alert, 27.9 s AI report, and a chat question asked during the incident answered in 6 s; each answer shows the engine that produced it). Model-answered chat questions took 7-14 s in fresh sessions (up to ~28 s for a long list), against 10-42 s on the GPU.

How an incident flows today
  1. Sensor readings arrive every 5 min (the demo replays real campus data, one reading every 2 s). Late, duplicate and missing readings are handled; silent sensors are reported as silent, never filled in.
  2. Critical fast path: 2 fresh readings past a critical limit open the incident at once (about 5.6 s; 2.6 s after a silent gap), severity critical, no AI wait.
  3. Preliminary report in the same second: rules from the live values, worded like the most similar past AI report (retrieval). Its checklist is live at once: the operator can acknowledge and tick steps while the AI writes.
  4. Event gate: 8 abnormal readings in a 4 h window start the AI: one domain agent per flagged sensor, all decoded together on the NPU.
  5. Reasoner report (rsn_v1) replaces the preliminary on the same incident, about 28 s after the fast incident in the latest check. "AI so far" shows its title and severity while it writes. The validator checks every field; a failing report falls back to a deterministic one.
  6. Re-checks at 1.5 h and 3 h, plus 12 h drift and level-shift checks, can raise, lower or resolve the incident. The operator approves every action.

Why it is this fast and not faster: decode reads every weight per token and sits at the memory-bandwidth roof (about 52 GB/s over 2.6 GB for the 4B), so we cut sequential steps (batching, split streams, verified drafts) rather than per-step cost; and a second model resident on the NPU rebooted the board, so the reasoner is swapped in. Real-world time is dominated by the 8-reading gate, which the critical fast path bypasses.

Models today

The 2B chats better; the 4B writes better reports. That is why the evaluation-day split exists, and why the 4B is the one being trained further.

Campus Assistant
  • Router first. A Go question-type layer answers most questions straight from read-only tools: counts, lists, current levels, faulty sensors, rankings, what-if against limits, period summaries, and follow-ups such as "which ones?".
  • Model for the rest, with up to 5 read-only tool calls, shown under "How I found this". On evaluation day the model is the 2B on the CPU, so chat no longer waits behind an incident analysis.
  • Hinglish and typos: kitne, aaj, kal, kharab, theek hai, ghante, din. "CO2 theek hai KH level 3 pe?" was answered from current_levels in under a second on the board.
  • Refusals: close, approve, dismiss, delete or reset requests and prompt injections are refused; invented incident ids and sensors are rejected by a guard.
  • Evaluation-day sweep: deploy30 (2B on the CPU), same 160 questions as deploy27: 160/160 answered, 0 errors, 0 busy; router 143/160 (median 0.2 s, as before); 17 model answers: mean 8.4-13.4 s per set vs 9.2-12.4 s on the 4B, except one 63 s loop (the 2B repeated a tool call; repeats are now dropped, deploy31). Read side by side (not yet graded against ground truth): the 2B is better on about 6 of 17 (e.g. "show me critical alerts": it lists them, the 4B said "no critical incidents"), worse on 1-2 (power spike), the rest equal or weak for both. Known limits
  • Sensor time dominates. A non-critical event reaches the AI after 8 readings, 40 to 55 min of real sensor time. Only sensors with a critical limit have the fast path; power, tank level and flow have no fixed limits.
  • Heat. Normal NPU work reaches 84 to 97 °C on the hottest zone, and back-to-back heavy incidents trip the thermal guard. The board needs a heatsink or fan in the field.
  • The NPU is not bit-for-bit repeatable. The same prompt can change wording near a decision boundary: severity agrees 97% of the time across repeats (100% with the guard).
  • Known model weaknesses. Recurring small spikes, pump cycling, and erratic or exponential shapes at 3 h are still often misnamed. Hourly sensors (such as TDS) have too few points and stay rules-only.
  • One NPU, two models. Two resident models rebooted the board, so each incident pays a model swap; bursts queue. On evaluation day the CPU carries the chat model so chat does not queue.
  • Two models for one job on evaluation day. The 2B is better at chat and worse at reports; until the 4B is better at both, the split stays an evaluation-only arrangement.
  • Chat. Blind questions still expose gaps; the "after" scores reuse the questions the fixes were designed from, which is why blind sets are written before each round.
  • Data. All models were trained and tested on one campus's sensors (test months held out, about 15% of sensors held out completely).
What's next
  • One general model. rsn_v3 4B (training on Kaggle) must beat rsn_v1 on reports and the 2B on chat; then the evaluation-day split goes away.
  • Younger events. Young-window training to shorten the 8-reading gate; the early provisional AI is built and off until 2 to 4 readings are proven.
  • Chat. A fresh blind set after every fix, streaming answers, and chat about the open incident.
  • Fog layer. Derived data to a free Supabase project with the board as the only writer; raw readings stay on the board.
Prototype → production

What a real deployment still needs. Security hardening is documented as a production to-do and is not implemented in the prototype; the other items are being built now and are not finished.

  • Security (documented, not built). The app reaches the NPU through the board's own debug bridge on 127.0.0.1:5555, which has no authentication. The app's HTTP API is plaintext and its reads, including per-room occupancy, are open on the local network. Production needs no debug bridge, authentication and TLS on every read, per-device keys, signed updates and an isolated network.
  • In progress. Real sensor ingestion over MQTT, time sync (the board's clock resets at boot), alerts that leave the board with escalation, a heartbeat with a dead-man alert, the fog uploader, early provisional AI with automatic warm-up, and over-the-air updates with automatic rollback.
  • Hardware. A DC supply with a small UPS, a heatsink and ventilated enclosure, and a soak test at 40 °C ambient.
  • Rollout. Two to four weeks in shadow mode (rules and the critical fast path alert people; AI reports are logged, not paged), one canary board, then a one-floor pilot with real sensors.
